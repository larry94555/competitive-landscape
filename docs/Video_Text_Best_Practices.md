# Landscape — Writing Demo Narration

> How the words are written. [Video_Guidelines.md](Video_Guidelines.md) covers the craft of
> *making* the film — captions, pacing, framing, chapters. This covers the harder part: what
> the sentences actually say.
>
> Written after a reviewer read the first three films and said the narration "completely lacks
> the salesperson's insight on how to show value without talking down." They were right. Every
> rule here comes with the line that failed it.

---

## 1. The standard

> **Every line is a mini elevator pitch: one idea, in the listener's currency, short enough
> that an impatient person does not tune out.**

An impatient person is the right reader to imagine. They did not come for a tour. They are
deciding, in about fifteen seconds, whether this is worth their afternoon. Every line either
earns the next fifteen seconds or spends them.

---

## 2. The "so what?" test

**After every line, ask "so what?" If there is no answer, cut the line or rewrite it until
there is.**

| Failed the test | Why | Rewritten |
|---|---|---|
| "Then the written part appears, a few words at a time." | So what? Nothing. It narrates pixels the viewer can already see. | "Then how each one pitches itself — in their words, not ours." |
| "First it works out what to call this line of business, then goes looking for companies already in it." | So what? That is plumbing. The viewer does not care *how*. | "Your words are not the market's words. It translates, then goes hunting." |
| "It reads each company's own website." | So what? *Because that is where prices are not spin.* Say the consequence. | "It reads what each company publishes about itself." |

**The most common failure is a true sentence with no consequence attached.** Accuracy is not
the bar. *Mattering* is the bar.

---

## 3. Value in the listener's currency

The viewer thinks in four currencies. Every line should trade in one:

| Currency | Sounds like |
|---|---|
| **Time** | "Quote them without checking." |
| **Embarrassment** | A wrong price in a pitch deck |
| **Money** | What it costs, what it finds |
| **Risk** | Being wrong in writing, in front of someone who matters |

**Never in the product's currency.** "Five cell states", "verified citations", "source
classes" are how the thing is built. Nobody buys a database schema.

---

## 4. Let them sell themselves

**The strongest line states a fact with an obvious implication, then stops.**

> "One doesn't publish a price. So neither do we."

Nine words. The narration never says *trustworthy*, *honest*, or *rigorous*. The viewer
supplies all three, and they believe it, because they got there themselves.

Compare the version that did the thinking for them:

> "One company does not publish a price at all, so the report says that instead of guessing."

Same fact, twice the words, and it hands over the conclusion — which makes it a claim rather
than a demonstration, and claims invite argument.

**The rule: show the receipt, stop talking.** If the implication is obvious, saying it out loud
is the one thing that can weaken it.

---

## 5. Never talk down

Banned outright, because each one implies the viewer might have struggled:

| Never | Why |
|---|---|
| "simply", "just", "easy" | If it is easy and they hesitated, you have insulted them |
| "all you have to do is" | Same |
| "That is the only thing you have to do." | Reassurance nobody asked for |
| "As you can see…" | They can see. That is the point. |
| "Don't worry" | Now they are worried |
| "powerful", "seamless", "robust", "intuitive" | Vendor words. They describe nothing and cost credibility |

**The tell:** if a line would sound condescending said to a peer across a table, it is
condescending in a demo.

---

## 6. Word budget

| | |
|---|---|
| Target | **10–18 words** |
| Hard ceiling | 25, and only when the idea genuinely needs it |
| One idea per line | Two ideas means two lines |

Spoken narration runs over moving pictures. It has to compete with what the eye is doing, so
it must be shorter than written prose, not the same length.

**Worked cut** — 33 words to 17:

> ~~"It is not the company's own figure, so it stays out of the table, but it is reported
> underneath with a link and a note saying why we could not confirm it."~~
>
> **"A blog quotes a price. Not from the company, so it sits outside the table — flagged,
> with a link."**

Same information. Half the words. Lands.

---

## 7. Concrete beats abstract, every time

Specificity *is* credibility. A real number is worth three adjectives.

| Abstract | Concrete |
|---|---|
| "Customer reviews are analyzed for unmet needs." | "Six people wanted per-restaurant pricing. Nobody sells it." |
| "It surfaces competitors you may not know." | "Three competitors you would never have found." |
| "Sources are timestamped and verifiable." | "The page, the sentence, the date." |

The concrete version is usually **shorter as well as better**, which is the tell that the
abstract one was hiding a lack of substance.

---

## 8. Do not narrate what is on screen

If the viewer can see it, saying it wastes a line. Narration should say **what it means**, not
what it looks like.

| Describing | Meaning |
|---|---|
| "Each page shows up as it is read." | "It reads what each company publishes about itself." |
| "A table appears comparing the three." | "A question mark means we looked and found nothing. It never means no." |

---

## 9. Earn the claim; never assert it

Every trust word the product might want to use — accurate, honest, verifiable, careful — is
**forbidden as an adjective and mandatory as a demonstration.**

- Do not say "every claim is verifiable." Show a citation opening, and say what is inside it.
- Do not say "it does not guess." Show one company with no published price and say "so neither
  do we."
- Do not say "it is careful about what it claims." Show the question mark and explain it.

A demonstrated property is believed. An asserted one is discounted, because every competitor
asserts the same thing.

---

## 10. Rhythm

Spoken lines have a shape, and a run of same-length sentences flattens into noise.

- **Vary the length.** A long line, a long line, then a four-word one. The short one lands.
- **Land the section on the short one.** "So neither do we." "Rivals are not neutral."
  "Nobody sells it."
- **No throat-clearing.** Cut "What this does is", "You will notice that", "It is worth
  mentioning". Start on the verb or the noun that matters.
- **Read it aloud.** If you run out of breath, or stumble, rewrite. This narration *is* spoken
  aloud, so a line that reads fine and speaks badly is a line that fails.
- **Contractions are fine.** "You'd never have found them" is how people talk. Avoiding
  apostrophes for technical convenience produced *"each company own website"* in an earlier
  cut — a self-inflicted wound that made the whole script read like a bad translation.

---

## 11. Structure across the film

- **Open on the problem, not the product.** The first line should make someone recognize
  their own situation. *"Who else is already doing what you're about to build?"*
- **First value inside 20 seconds.** Something useful has to happen before the viewer decides
  this is a tour.
- **Put the strongest moment where it is earned, not where it is convenient.** Here that is
  three competitors the viewer has never heard of — it proves the hardest capability, and
  everything after it is easier to believe.
- **End on the viewer, not the product.** "Now try it out yourself" beats any summary of
  features.

---

## 12. The rewrite loop

For each line, in order. It takes a couple of minutes and it is worth it:

1. **So what?** No answer → cut it or find the consequence.
2. **Whose currency?** Time, embarrassment, money, or risk. If none, rewrite.
3. **Am I finishing their thought?** If the implication is obvious, delete the conclusion.
4. **Count the words.** Over 18, cut. Over 25, split.
5. **Concrete?** Swap any abstraction for the actual number or name.
6. **Condescending to a peer?** Remove the softener.
7. **Say it out loud.** Stumble → rewrite.

---

## 13. Checklist

Blocking, alongside the checklist in [Video_Guidelines.md](Video_Guidelines.md) §10:

- [ ] Every line survives "so what?"
- [ ] Every line trades in time, embarrassment, money, or risk.
- [ ] No line asserts a trust word that the picture could demonstrate instead.
- [ ] No line finishes a thought the viewer would reach unaided.
- [ ] Nothing from §5. No "simply", "just", "easy", "seamless", "powerful".
- [ ] 10–18 words typical; nothing over 25.
- [ ] One idea per line.
- [ ] No line describes what is plainly visible.
- [ ] Each section lands on a short sentence.
- [ ] Read aloud start to finish without stumbling.
- [ ] Opens on the problem. Ends on the viewer.
